\title{\textbf{Amortized Bayesian parameter recovery for dynamical systems\\ via conditional flow matching}\\[2mm]
\large Technical report on the \amortix\ package, v0.1}
\author{Ivan Oseledets}
\date{August 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We consider the inverse problem of recovering the parameters of a dynamical system --- an ODE, an SDE, or a
PDE --- from noisy observations of its trajectory at a finite set of time points, in the Bayesian setting:
the answer is the posterior distribution over parameters, not a point estimate. The \amortix\ package
implements an \emph{amortized} solver: a single neural network is trained once, on simulated
(parameters, data) pairs, and thereafter produces posterior samples for any new dataset in milliseconds,
with no likelihood evaluations and no per-dataset optimization. The generative engine is conditional flow
matching: the network learns a velocity field that transports a standard Gaussian onto the posterior,
conditioned on the data. A single trained model handles \emph{any number and placement of observation
points} --- from a handful of irregular measurements to near-continuous monitoring --- which is the setting
required for sequential experiment refinement and optimal design of measurements. This report gives the
problem setup and the method from scratch, describes the architecture in plain terms, defines the evaluation
protocol, and summarizes benchmark results on fifteen systems, including a stochastic-volatility model with
a hidden state, a jump-diffusion, the Hodgkin--Huxley neuron, a pharmacokinetic model, and a
reaction--diffusion PDE. On the fixed-design benchmark, 31 of 32 parameters across nine systems pass strict
calibration tests; on the variable-design zoo, posteriors agree with exact-likelihood references up to
residuals that decay smoothly with training compute. We also document, in plain language, the three failure
modes we encountered --- all traceable to the conditioning of the encoder's input --- and their cures.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem setup}
\label{sec:setup}

Let a dynamical system depend on an unknown parameter vector $m \in \R^{d}$ with a known prior $p(m)$
(in all our benchmarks, uniform on a box). The system produces a state trajectory $x(t)$ --- through an ODE
$\dot{x} = f(x; m)$, an SDE $dx = f(x;m)\,dt + \sigma(x;m)\,dW_t$, or a discretized PDE --- and we observe
it through a noisy measurement channel at $K$ time points $t_1 < \dots < t_K$:
\begin{equation}
  y_i = h\bigl(x(t_i)\bigr) + \varepsilon_i, \qquad
  \D = \{(t_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{K}.
\end{equation}
The observation operator $h$ may hide part of the state (e.g.\ only the price is seen while the volatility
is latent; only the voltage of a neuron is seen while the gating variables are not; only three spatial
sensors of a PDE field are read). The goal is the posterior
\begin{equation}
  p(m \mid \D) \;\propto\; p(\D \mid m)\, p(m).
\end{equation}

Two features make this hard for classical machinery. First, for most systems above the likelihood
$p(\D \mid m)$ is intractable or expensive: it requires integrating out the latent path between
observations. Second, we want the answer \emph{fast and repeatedly}: in calibration studies, in sequential
experiments, and in design-of-experiments loops, the posterior must be evaluated for thousands of datasets
and for many candidate subsets of measurement points.

\paragraph{Amortization.} Simulation-based (likelihood-free) inference sidesteps both issues. Sampling from
the joint is easy --- draw $m_j \sim p(m)$, simulate the system, apply the observation channel --- and
yields training pairs $(m_j, \D_j)$. A conditional generative model $q_\theta(m \mid \D)$ trained on these
pairs approximates the posterior \emph{for every dataset simultaneously}: the cost of inference is moved
into a one-time training phase (hence ``amortized''). At deployment, posterior sampling is a forward pass.

\paragraph{Design amortization.} We additionally require that a \emph{single} trained network be correct
for any number $K$ and any placement of observation times (and, for PDEs, sensor positions). Formally, if
$S$ is a subset of the measurement points of one experiment, the network must approximate $p(m \mid S)$
coherently for \emph{all} subsets $S$ --- e.g.\ the posterior must widen honestly when points are removed.
This is precisely the object needed for optimal experimental design, where candidate designs are compared
by the posteriors they would produce.

\section{Method: posterior sampling by conditional flow matching}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Parameter normalization.} Since the prior is a product of known one-dimensional distributions,
we map parameters componentwise through their prior CDF and the standard normal quantile,
\begin{equation}
  z = \Phi^{-1}\!\bigl(F_{\text{prior}}(m)\bigr) \in \R^{d},
  \label{eq:probit}
\end{equation}
so that in $z$-coordinates the \emph{prior} is exactly $\N(0, I)$ and box constraints disappear. This choice
has a practical consequence: when the data carry no information, the posterior equals the prior equals the
base distribution, and the model has nothing to learn --- the uninformative limit is the zero of the
parametrization, not a nontrivial target.

\paragraph{Flow matching.} We represent the posterior as the endpoint of a learned transport. Let
$z_0 \sim \N(0, I)$ be base noise and $z_1$ the (normalized) parameter drawn jointly with its dataset $\D$.
Connect them by the straight line
\begin{equation}
  z_t = (1 - t)\, z_0 + t\, z_1, \qquad t \in [0, 1],
\end{equation}
and train a velocity network $v_\theta$ to regress the direction of this line, conditioned on the data:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\theta) \;=\;
  \E_{\,m,\D \sim p(m)p(\D \mid m),\; z_0 \sim \N(0,I),\; t \sim U[0,1]}
  \;\bigl\| v_\theta(z_t,\, t,\, \D) - (z_1 - z_0) \bigr\|^2 .
  \label{eq:cfm}
\end{equation}
By the conditional flow matching theorem \cite{lipman2023}, the minimizer of \eqref{eq:cfm} is the
\emph{marginal} velocity field whose flow transports $\N(0, I)$ at $t=0$ onto the conditional distribution
$p(z_1 \mid \D)$ at $t=1$ --- i.e.\ onto the posterior in $z$-coordinates. Intuitively: the network sees a
cloud of straight lines from noise to posterior samples and learns their average direction at every
$(z, t, \D)$; following that averaged field deforms the Gaussian cloud into the posterior cloud.

\paragraph{Sampling.} Given a new dataset $\D$, draw $z_0 \sim \N(0,I)$, integrate
$\dot z = v_\theta(z, t, \D)$ from $t = 0$ to $1$ (we use 20 midpoint steps; finer solvers were tested and
change nothing, Sec.~\ref{sec:budget}), and map back through the inverse of \eqref{eq:probit}. Repeating
with fresh $z_0$ gives i.i.d.\ posterior samples. Evaluation uses an exponential moving average of the
training weights.

Note what is \emph{not} required: no likelihood, no adjoint of the simulator, no per-dataset MCMC or
optimization. The simulator is only ever run forward, during training-set generation.

\section{Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

The network has two parts: an \emph{encoder} that turns the observation set $\D$ into context vectors, and
the \emph{velocity network} $v_\theta$ that consumes them. The design brief for the encoder was strict
universality: no hand-crafted, problem-specific features; every transformation of the raw data must be
learnable and shared across all problems. The default model is small --- about $5 \times 10^5$ weights
(width 64, three encoder blocks and three velocity blocks) --- and Sec.~\ref{sec:budget} shows capacity is
not the accuracy bottleneck.

\subsection{From observations to tokens}

Each observation becomes a token (a feature vector). Two constructions are used, chosen by an explicit and
empirically verified rule.

\paragraph{Learnable axis reparametrization.} Both constructions share one module: a monotone map
$w(\cdot)$ applied to observed values (and to time gaps), parametrized as a positive mixture over a bank of
signed-logarithmic scales $\{\,\mathrm{sign}(x) \log(1 + |x| / s_k)\,\}$ with learnable weights. The
mixture spans the identity and the logarithm continuously, so the network can \emph{learn} the coordinate
in which the process is well-conditioned (e.g.\ log-price for geometric Brownian motion) instead of having
it prescribed. A cautionary measurement guided this design: a more general Yeo--Johnson transform also
contains the logarithm in its parameter space (at $\lambda = 0$), yet gradient descent never found it ---
membership in the hypothesis class is not the same as reachability from the initialization. The mixture
parametrization keeps the useful coordinates inside the basin that gradients actually explore.

\paragraph{Point tokens (the general case).} Each observation contributes the token
\[
  \bigl[\,t_i,\; w(y_i),\; \text{channel/sensor id},\; \text{set-size feature}\,\bigr].
\]
Nothing else is assumed about the process.

\paragraph{Pair tokens (when the observed process is Markov).} If the \emph{observed} coordinate is itself
Markov --- as for scalar SDEs observed directly --- the likelihood factorizes over consecutive observations,
$p(\D \mid m) = \prod_i p\bigl(y_{i+1} \mid y_i;\, m\bigr)$, and the sufficient arguments of each factor
are the pair $(y_i, y_{i+1})$ and the gap $\Delta t_i$. We exploit this by building one token per
consecutive pair (after sorting by time):
$\bigl[\,t_i,\; w(y_i),\; \Delta w_i,\; (\Delta w_i)^2,\; w_t(\Delta t_i)\,\bigr]$, where
$\Delta w_i = w(y_{i+1}) - w(y_i)$. These are exactly the inputs a transition density needs, presented in a
learnable coordinate --- an inductive bias, not a hand-crafted statistic. Two additions make this
construction robust: squared increments are logarithmically compressed before normalization (heavy-tailed
processes such as jump-diffusions otherwise destroy the feature scaling --- Sec.~\ref{sec:anatomy}), and a
set-conditioning block (statistics pooled over the whole token set modulate each feature channel, with the
modulation initialized to the identity) lets the normalization adapt to each dataset.

\paragraph{The class rule.} Pair tokens are used \emph{iff} the observed process is Markov; point tokens
otherwise. The rule was verified in both directions: a pair-token model trained on one Markov system
transfers cleanly to another (geometric Brownian motion $\to$ Ornstein--Uhlenbeck), while on a
stochastic-volatility model, where the observed price is \emph{not} Markov (the volatility is hidden), the
pair construction loses its advantage entirely --- point and pair models become statistically
indistinguishable, as the factorization argument predicts. The package selects the construction
automatically from a per-problem Markovianity flag.

\subsection{Set encoding: time, permutation invariance, and set size}

The tokens are processed by a transformer encoder. Three details matter and were each isolated by
experiment.

\paragraph{Time enters through attention phases.} Standard rotary position embeddings encode the
\emph{index} of a token, which is meaningless for irregularly sampled data --- and worse than meaningless:
we measured that index-based encoding creates a hidden train/test contract on token placement, producing
systematic biases of $1$--$3$ posterior standard deviations with no code being ``wrong''. Instead, the
attention phases are computed from \emph{physical time} $t_i$ through a geometric bank of frequencies. The
result is exact permutation invariance of the encoder (verified to $10^{-8}$): the model sees a \emph{set}
of time-stamped measurements, not a sequence.

\paragraph{Normalized attention cannot count.} Posterior \emph{width} is governed by the number of
observations $K$ (in regular problems, error $\sim K^{-1/2}$), but softmax attention computes weighted
\emph{means} over the set --- and means erase cardinality. We measured this directly: linear probes on the
encoder's pooled output recover the posterior-mean statistics with $R^2 = 0.83$--$0.94$ in every
architecture variant, while $R^2(\log K)$ collapses exactly in the variants whose posterior widths fail.
The cure is one bit of honesty, not process knowledge: append $\log K$ as a feature (or let two learned
``register'' tokens accumulate the count). With it, dense-design widths drop from ratio $\approx 1.95$ to
$1.49$ at $K = 100$ without touching bias.

\paragraph{Velocity network.} The current flow state $z_t$ is processed by self-attention over the $d$
parameter slots; the flow time $t$ and the pooled data context modulate every block through adaptive layer
norms whose modulation is initialized to zero (adaLN-Zero, \cite{peebles2023}) --- training starts from an
identity-like map and departs only as the data warrant.

\section{Training protocol for design amortization}
\label{sec:recipe}

Training data are simulated on a fine grid once; observation designs are then \emph{resampled from the
stored fine paths at every optimizer step}. The recipe has three components, each fixed by a controlled
comparison (systems: geometric Brownian motion, Ornstein--Uhlenbeck, FitzHugh--Nagumo):

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Fresh designs every step.} Each trajectory's observation subset is re-drawn at every
  optimizer step. If instead each trajectory keeps one fixed design, a long training run fits the dense
  designs but \emph{overfits the design set}: at sparse $K$ the model becomes overconfident (posterior
  width ratio down to $0.86$ against the exact reference). Fresh resampling makes design memorization
  impossible, and one simulation serves unboundedly many designs --- which is also exactly the training
  mode that makes $p(m \mid S)$ coherent across subsets $S$, as required for experiment design.
  \item \textbf{The size distribution of designs.} $K$ is drawn 50\% log-uniform on $[K_{\min}, K_{\max}]$
  and 50\% uniform on the dense half of the range. Log-uniform alone starves the dense regime (only
  $\sim 6\%$ of mass lands at $K \ge 100$), which showed up as inflated widths precisely there; the mixture
  alone halves the dense-design excess (e.g.\ width ratio $1.27 \to 1.14$ on geometric Brownian motion).
  \item \textbf{Budget scaling.} When more accuracy is needed, the number of simulations and optimizer
  steps are scaled \emph{together} (constant $\approx 38$ epochs). Section~\ref{sec:budget} shows the
  residuals then decay as clean power laws.
\end{enumerate}

With this recipe, on geometric Brownian motion the posterior width matches the exact per-design posterior
to within $5$--$7\%$ uniformly over $K = 5, 9, 20, 100$.

\section{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Two instruments are used, with distinct roles.

\paragraph{Simulation-based calibration (the screen).} SBC \cite{talts2018} checks a necessary condition of
Bayesian correctness: if $m^\ast \sim p(m)$, $\D \sim p(\D \mid m^\ast)$, then the rank of $m^\ast$ among
posterior samples must be uniform; departures are tested per parameter (we report the p-value of a uniformity
test; $p > 0.05$ passes). SBC needs no reference posterior and runs on every system, but its statistical
power at practical sizes is limited, and we measured the limit: at $300$--$500$ simulated datasets it is
blind to posterior-width errors up to $\approx 30\%$ and its p-values flicker between retrains when residual
biases are $0.1$--$0.2$ posterior-sd. It also cannot penalize a posterior that simply returns the prior.
SBC is therefore used as a cheap screen, never as the final verdict.

\paragraph{Reference posteriors (the record).} Wherever a tractable likelihood exists we compare
\emph{distributions} on the same observed points: the signed error of the posterior mean in units of the
reference posterior's standard deviation (``bias''), and the ratio of standard deviations (``width'',
$1$ = perfect, $>1$ = conservative, $<1$ = overconfident). References are closed-form where available
(geometric Brownian motion, linear-Gaussian) and otherwise adaptive Metropolis MCMC with exact likelihoods
(a Poisson-mixture transition density for the jump-diffusion; log-normal residuals around the
pharmacokinetic curve; Gaussian noise around the PDE solve). Chains are validated by multi-start agreement
before being trusted; where both a chain and a closed form exist, the chains reproduce the truth to worst
mean discrepancy $0.099$ posterior-sd and widths $0.91$--$1.03$ over 24 comparisons.

Twice during development the two instruments contradicted each other, and both times the contradiction
exposed a real bug (a padding mask leaking into set-level statistics; the index-based positional contract
of Sec.~\ref{sec:arch}). Two disagreeing instruments are worth more than either alone.

\paragraph{Encoder forensics.} A third diagnostic separates \emph{extraction} failures from
\emph{downstream} failures: linear (ridge) probes from the encoder's pooled context onto the problem's known
sufficient statistics. In the jump-diffusion autopsy the failing model encoded the robust statistics
\emph{better} than the healthy one ($R^2 = 0.967$ on truncated realized variance) --- the information was
extracted, and the defect was in the representation's stability, not its content. This redirected the fix
(Sec.~\ref{sec:anatomy}).

\section{Benchmark results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Fixed-design gallery: nine systems}

Nine systems with a shared observation grid per problem: linear-Gaussian (sanity case with an exact
posterior), Ornstein--Uhlenbeck, geometric Brownian motion, Cox--Ingersoll--Ross, a double-well SDE,
stochastic Lotka--Volterra, FitzHugh--Nagumo, SEIR, and an SDE with a cubic drift to be discovered
(SINDy-style). Across the $32$ parameters, $31$ pass SBC; the single miss sits at the threshold
($p = 0.04$). Mean calibration error is $1.3$ percentage points. On the cases with exact references, the
learned posteriors extract $89$--$90\%$ of the available information (width vs.\ the exact posterior).

\subsection{Residuals decay as power laws in training compute}
\label{sec:budget}

The two stubborn residuals of the zoo were driven down by scaling training budget (simulations and steps
together, Sec.~\ref{sec:recipe}) at \emph{fixed} model size:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
 & $\times 1$ budget & $\times 3$ & $\times 6$ \\
\midrule
jump-diffusion: width excess, hardest cell & $0.246$ & $0.120$ & $0.059$ \\
reaction--diffusion: along-ridge bias (sd) & $+0.41$ & $+0.23$ & $+0.14$ \\
reaction--diffusion: front-speed bias (sd) & $-0.21$ & $-0.14$ & $-0.075$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The width excess decays $\propto 1/\text{budget}$; the ridge bias $\sim \text{budget}^{-1/2}$ --- the
weakly identified direction of the posterior (weakest gradient signal in the loss \eqref{eq:cfm}) converges
last, with no wall. Two negative controls complete the picture: doubling network width and depth at fixed
budget changes almost nothing ($1.103$ vs.\ $1.120$), and refining the sampling ODE solver (RK4 with 60
steps vs.\ midpoint with 20) changes nothing at all. Remaining inaccuracy is a compute price list, not an
open problem.

\section{Failure anatomy: one disease, three guises}
\label{sec:anatomy}

Every hard failure we encountered across fifteen systems reduced to the same cause: the
\emph{conditioning of the encoder's input}. It appeared in three guises, and each cure is a learnable,
problem-agnostic module:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
guise & symptom & cure \\
\midrule
floating multiplicative scale & $+0.48$\,sd bias on volatility & learnable monotone axis map (Sec.~\ref{sec:arch}) \\
set-size blindness & widths $1.5$--$2.5\times$ at dense designs & explicit $\log K$ / register tokens \\
heavy tails (jumps) & squared increments, sd $3 \times 10^4$ & log-compression + set conditioning \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The third guise deserves one paragraph, because its autopsy illustrates the method of the whole project.
On the jump-diffusion, the pair-token model failed with a $+1.12$\,sd bias. Probing (Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol})
showed the relevant statistics were extracted \emph{better} than in healthy models --- the failure was that
jump outliers set the running normalization scale (standard deviation $3 \times 10^4$ against a diffusion
signal of order $10^{-2}$--$1$), so the flow trained against a drifting representation. Log-compressing the
increment features (near-identity on clean diffusions, since $\log(1+x) \approx x$ for small $x$) fixed all
25 calibration cells. A precision probe then found a smaller residual ($+0.27$\,sd) with its own mechanism:
the effective jump/no-jump classification threshold floats with the unknown volatility. Here we raced a
hand-crafted fix (per-dataset median rescaling) against a universal learned one (set-pooled statistics
modulating feature channels, initialized to identity): $+0.094 \pm 0.054$ vs.\ $+0.127 \pm 0.055$ ---
statistically indistinguishable. This was the third such duel of the project, and the third draw. The
emerging pattern: \emph{the hand-crafted fix identifies the mechanism and the right initialization basin;
the learnable module placed in that basin is what ships.}

\section{Baselines: what any result here is compared against}
\label{sec:baselines}

Every claim is read against a ladder of comparisons (shipped in the package; details in
\texttt{BASELINES.md}):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Controls.} Predicting the prior mean (exactly $25\%$ mean absolute error on a uniform
  prior) and a degree-2 ridge regression on $\sim 20$ generic summary statistics --- the cheapest serious
  estimator. These also unmask a failure mode SBC cannot see: a posterior that returns the prior passes SBC
  and fails both controls.
  \item \textbf{Classical per-case estimators.} Each benchmark problem ships the estimator a domain
  practitioner would use: exact maximum likelihood (Ornstein--Uhlenbeck, geometric Brownian motion), Euler
  pseudo-likelihood (Cox--Ingersoll--Ross), Kramers--Moyal / SINDy regression (drift discovery), multi-start
  nonlinear least squares (FitzHugh--Nagumo, SEIR, Lotka--Volterra), the exact posterior mean
  (linear-Gaussian).
  \item \textbf{Reference posteriors.} Closed forms and validated exact-likelihood MCMC
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol}) --- the instrument of record.
  \item \textbf{External context.} BayesFlow \cite{radev2020}, the \texttt{sbi} package, and the
  \texttt{sbibm} benchmark suite \cite{lueckmann2021}, with which our Hodgkin--Huxley and
  ecology/epidemiology cases deliberately overlap. The differentiators of \amortix\ are the SDE-first
  problem zoo with real numerical solvers, single-model variable-design amortization, and the
  exact-reference evaluation harness.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations and outlook}

(i) Residual widths and ridge biases lie on measured power-law budget curves; sharpening them is a compute
purchase, not a research question. (ii) The power limits of SBC mean that small residuals can only be
certified where an exact or validated reference exists; systems without tractable likelihoods inherit the
weaker screen. (iii) Optimal experimental design is the natural next step: the training mode of
Sec.~\ref{sec:recipe} already makes $p(m \mid S)$ coherent across subsets $S$ of one experiment, which is
the required primitive. (iv) Real-data studies with posterior predictive checks are not yet included.
