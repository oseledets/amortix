\begin{abstract}
We describe \amortix, a package for amortized Bayesian estimation of the parameters of dynamical systems
--- ordinary and stochastic differential equations and discretized PDEs --- from noisy observations at a
finite set of time points. A conditional flow-matching model is trained once on simulated
(parameter, data) pairs and thereafter produces posterior samples for any new dataset without likelihood
evaluations or per-dataset optimization; a single trained network handles any number and placement of
observation points, the setting required for sequential experiment refinement and Bayesian experimental
design. The package couples three components that we argue are only useful together: (i)~a set-transformer
encoder whose input representation is learnable rather than prescribed --- a monotone mixture-of-scales
reparametrization of the value axis, attention phases computed from physical observation times, and
explicit set-cardinality features; (ii)~a benchmark suite of fifteen systems spanning finance, neuroscience,
epidemiology, ecology, pharmacokinetics, classical mechanics, and reaction--diffusion PDEs; and (iii)~an
evaluation harness in which simulation-based calibration is used only as a screen, and the instrument of
record is comparison against exact or validated Markov chain Monte Carlo reference posteriors on the same
observed points. On the fixed-design suite, 31 of 32 parameters across nine systems pass simulation-based
calibration with a mean coverage error of $1.3$ percentage points; on the variable-design suite, posterior
widths match exact references to within $5$--$7\%$ uniformly over design sizes $K = 5$--$100$, and the two
residual defects found anywhere in the suite decay as measured power laws of the training budget. We
compare against neural posterior estimation and flow-matching posterior estimation from the \texttt{sbi}
package, both network families of BayesFlow~2, and a port of the Simformer, on three systems with exact or
validated references. On a multiplicative process, all external configurations either inflate the
volatility posterior by factors of $1.4$--$3.2$ or become overconfident (widths $0.4$--$0.9$) depending on
the input coordinates they are given, while the learnable representation recovers the exact posterior from
raw data. On an additive control process the same external tools improve markedly --- the systematic
bias disappears and the width inflation shrinks from $2.5$--$3.2$ to $1.6$--$1.9$ --- which localizes the
dominant failure to input conditioning while showing that a residual representation gap remains even in
the benign case. We document this failure mechanism --- the
conditioning of the encoder input --- in three distinct forms, together with learnable modules that repair
each one.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Estimating the parameters of a differential-equation model from noisy, irregularly sampled trajectory data
is among the most common inverse problems in applied science. Two features make the fully Bayesian version
of this problem expensive. First, for most stochastic and partially observed systems, the likelihood
$p(\D \mid m)$ requires integrating over latent paths between observations and is intractable or costly.
Second, modern workflows evaluate posteriors \emph{repeatedly}: calibration studies process thousands of
synthetic datasets; sequential experiments update the posterior after every new measurement; Bayesian
experimental design compares candidate observation schedules by the posteriors they would produce.
Per-dataset MCMC or optimization inside such loops is the dominant cost even when a likelihood is
available, and is impossible when it is not.

Simulation-based inference (SBI) addresses both issues by learning a conditional generative model
$q_\theta(m \mid \D)$ from simulated pairs $(m_j, \D_j)$, amortizing the cost of inference into a one-time
training phase \citep{cranmer2020}. Neural posterior estimation (NPE) and its sequential variants
\citep{papamakarios2016, greenberg2019} fit normalizing flows by maximum likelihood; flow-matching and
diffusion variants \citep{wildberger2023, gloeckler2024} replace the density estimator with transport-based
generative models. Mature software exists: the \texttt{sbi} toolkit \citep{boelts2024}, BayesFlow
\citep{radev2020}, and the \texttt{sbibm} benchmark collection \citep{lueckmann2021}.

This report describes \amortix, a compact package (about $3{,}500$ lines, PyTorch, one small default model
of $4.8 \times 10^5$ weights) built around three design decisions that distinguish it from the packages
above.

\paragraph{1. The input representation is learned, not chosen.} Conditional density estimators are
routinely fitted to a fixed data vector after global standardization. Section~\ref{sec:anatomy} shows,
across four different generative engines from three codebases, that this convention fails on multiplicative
and heavy-tailed processes in a specific, reproducible way, and that supplying hand-derived coordinates
trades one failure mode for others (over-dispersion, overconfidence, or bias, depending on the package).
\amortix\ instead makes the coordinate system part of the model: a learnable monotone reparametrization of
the value axis (Sec.~\ref{sec:warp}), observation times entering attention as continuous phases
(Sec.~\ref{sec:trope}), and explicit set-cardinality features (Sec.~\ref{sec:count}). Each component is
motivated by a measured failure and validated by an ablation.

\paragraph{2. One network for all observation designs.} \amortix\ trains a single model to approximate
$p(m \mid S)$ coherently for \emph{every} subset $S$ of measurement points of an experiment --- arbitrary
sizes and placements, including spatio-temporal sensor designs for PDEs
(Sec.~\ref{sec:design-amortization}). This is the primitive required for sequential refinement and
experimental design, and it changes the training procedure (Sec.~\ref{sec:recipe}): observation designs
must be resampled fresh at every optimizer step, and the distribution of design sizes becomes a modeling
choice with measurable consequences.

\paragraph{3. Calibration screening is separated from verification.} Simulation-based calibration (SBC)
\citep{talts2018} is a necessary-condition test with limited power at practical sample sizes; we measure
that limit (Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol}) and treat SBC only as a screen. The instrument of record throughout is
the comparison of posterior mean and width against an exact or validated-MCMC reference posterior computed
on the same observed points. The package ships the reference machinery --- closed-form posteriors where
they exist, exact-likelihood adaptive Metropolis samplers elsewhere, and chain-validation diagnostics ---
as part of its public API. The concern that motivates this discipline is documented in the SBI literature:
learned posteriors can be confidently wrong in ways that rank-based diagnostics do not detect
\citep{hermans2022}.

The remainder of the report presents the method (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}), the architecture
(Sec.~\ref{sec:arch}), training procedures (Sec.~\ref{sec:recipe}), the evaluation methodology
(Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol}), the benchmark suite (Sec.~\ref{sec:suite}), experimental results including
comparisons with \texttt{sbi}, BayesFlow, and the Simformer on three systems with exact or validated
references (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}), an analysis of the failure modes encountered during development
(Sec.~\ref{sec:anatomy}), and limitations (Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}). Appendix~\ref{app:systems} defines
every benchmark system with its equations, priors, and observation model; Appendix~\ref{app:refs} details
the reference posteriors; Appendix~\ref{app:package} maps the terminology of this report to package
options.

\section{Problem formulation}
\label{sec:setup}

Let a dynamical system depend on a parameter vector $m \in \R^{d}$ with a known prior $p(m)$, uniform on a
box in all benchmarks below. The system produces a state trajectory $x(t)$ --- through an ODE
$\dot{x} = f(x; m)$, an SDE $dx = f(x;m)\,dt + \sigma(x;m)\,dW_t$, or a spatially discretized PDE --- and
the trajectory is observed through a noisy channel at $K$ points:
\begin{equation}
  y_i = h\bigl(x(t_i)\bigr) + \varepsilon_i, \qquad
  \D = \{(t_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{K}.
  \label{eq:obs}
\end{equation}
The observation operator $h$ may expose only part of the state: in the benchmarks below, only the price of
an asset while its stochastic volatility is latent, only the membrane voltage of a neuron while its gating
variables are latent, or only three spatial sensors of a PDE field. The target is the posterior
$p(m \mid \D) \propto p(\D \mid m)\, p(m)$.

\paragraph{Amortization.} Sampling from the joint distribution is easy --- draw $m_j \sim p(m)$, simulate,
apply the observation channel --- and yields training pairs $(m_j, \D_j)$. A conditional generative model
trained on such pairs approximates the posterior for every dataset simultaneously; inference for a new
dataset is a forward pass.

\paragraph{Design amortization.} We additionally require that a single trained network be correct for any
number $K$ and placement of observation times (and sensor positions). Formally, for every subset $S$ of the
measurement points of one experiment, the network must approximate $p(m \mid S)$; in particular the
posterior must widen consistently as points are removed. This object is what sequential refinement and
Bayesian experimental design consume: candidate designs are compared by the posteriors they would produce.

\subsection{Challenges}
\label{sec:hard}

Five properties of this problem class shaped the design of the package; each defeated at least one
plausible architecture during development.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Intractable likelihoods.} For SDEs observed at gaps, the likelihood integrates over latent
  paths; for hidden-state models even the observed-process transition density is unavailable. Classical
  estimators exist per problem but each is a separate derivation (Appendix~\ref{app:systems} lists the one
  we implement for every benchmark system).
  \item \emph{The width of the posterior must be right.} A point estimate can be checked against the truth;
  a posterior asserts an uncertainty, and the two failure directions are asymmetric in consequence: an
  overconfident posterior is silently wrong for downstream decisions. Verifying width requires reference
  posteriors or calibration tests, and the standard calibration test has limited power
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol}), so detecting failure is itself nontrivial \citep{hermans2022}.
  \item \emph{One network must serve all designs.} Posterior width is governed by the number of
  observations $K$, yet softmax attention computes normalized averages that are nearly invariant to $K$
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:count}); and the training distribution over designs acquires measurable consequences
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:recipe}).
  \item \emph{Conditioning of the raw data.} Multiplicative processes have a per-dataset floating scale;
  jump processes produce squared-increment features whose sample standard deviation exceeds the median
  scale by six orders of magnitude; the coordinate in which the process is well-behaved (often the
  logarithm) differs per problem. Section~\ref{sec:anatomy} traces every hard failure encountered in this
  project to this property.
  \item \emph{Weak identifiability.} Posteriors of real systems concentrate on ridges. In the
  Fisher--KPP benchmark only the front speed $2\sqrt{Dr}$ is sharply determined, and the trained model's
  residual error concentrates along the flat direction of the posterior, where the training signal is
  weakest (Sec.~\ref{sec:budget}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Posterior sampling by conditional flow matching}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Parameter normalization.} Since the prior factorizes with known one-dimensional CDFs, parameters
are mapped componentwise through
\begin{equation}
  z = \Phi^{-1}\!\bigl(F_{\text{prior}}(m)\bigr) \in \R^{d},
  \label{eq:probit}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF. In $z$-coordinates the prior is exactly $\N(0, I)$ and box
constraints disappear. The choice fixes the uninformative limit: when the data carry no information the
posterior coincides with the base distribution and the transport below reduces to the identity, so the
network need not learn anything to represent ignorance correctly.

\paragraph{Conditional flow matching.} The posterior is represented as the endpoint of a learned transport
\citep{lipman2023}. Let $z_0 \sim \N(0, I)$ and let $z_1$ be the normalized parameter drawn jointly with
its dataset $\D$. Define the linear interpolation $z_t = (1 - t) z_0 + t z_1$ and train a velocity network
by regression:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\theta) =
  \E_{\,m,\D,\; z_0 \sim \N(0,I),\; t \sim U[0,1]}
  \bigl\| v_\theta(z_t,\, t,\, \D) - (z_1 - z_0) \bigr\|^2 .
  \label{eq:cfm}
\end{equation}
The minimizer of \eqref{eq:cfm} is the marginal velocity field whose flow transports $\N(0, I)$ at $t = 0$
to $p(z_1 \mid \D)$ at $t = 1$ \citep{lipman2023}. Sampling integrates $\dot z = v_\theta(z, t, \D)$ with
20 midpoint steps (a finer RK4/60 solver changes no benchmark result; Sec.~\ref{sec:budget}) and maps back
through the inverse of \eqref{eq:probit}. Evaluation uses an exponential moving average of the weights.
The same estimator family, with a different conditioning architecture, is available in \texttt{sbi} as
FMPE \citep{wildberger2023}; the comparison of Sec.~\ref{sec:headtohead} therefore isolates the effect of
the input representation from the choice of generative engine.

No likelihood evaluations, simulator gradients, or per-dataset optimization are required at any stage; the
simulator is executed only forward, during training-set generation.

\section{Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

The network consists of a set encoder that maps the observation set $\D$ to context vectors and a velocity
network that consumes them. The default model has width $64$, three encoder blocks, three velocity blocks,
and $4.8 \times 10^5$ parameters in total; Sec.~\ref{sec:budget} shows that capacity is not the accuracy
bottleneck on any benchmark, so the default is deliberately small. Throughout, the design constraint is
that no transformation of the raw data be problem-specific.

\subsection{Tokenization: point tokens and pair tokens}
\label{sec:tokens}

Each observation contributes one token. Two constructions are used.

\paragraph{Point tokens (general case).} Observation $i$ becomes
$\bigl[\,t_i,\; w(y_i),\; \text{sensor id},\; \text{set-size feature}\,\bigr]$, where $w$ is the learnable
reparametrization of Sec.~\ref{sec:warp}. Nothing is assumed about the process.

\paragraph{Pair tokens (Markov observations).} When the \emph{observed} coordinate is itself a Markov
process --- scalar SDEs observed directly --- the likelihood factorizes over consecutive observations, and
the factorization dictates a sufficient token content.

\begin{proposition}
Let the observed process be Markov and let $t_{(1)} < \dots < t_{(K)}$ be the sorted observation times.
Then $p(\D \mid m) = p(y_{(1)} \mid m) \prod_{i=1}^{K-1} p\bigl(y_{(i+1)} \mid y_{(i)}; m\bigr)$, and each
factor depends only on the pair $(y_{(i)}, y_{(i+1)})$ and the gap $\Delta t_{(i)}$.
\end{proposition}

Pair tokens present exactly these arguments in learnable coordinates,
\[
  \bigl[\,t_{(i)},\; w(y_{(i)}),\; \Delta w_i,\; (\Delta w_i)^2,\; w_t(\Delta t_{(i)})\,\bigr],
  \qquad \Delta w_i = w(y_{(i+1)}) - w(y_{(i)}),
\]
where the squared increment supplies the quadratic-variation
statistic that diffusion coefficients require. Two additions make the construction robust on heavy-tailed
processes (Sec.~\ref{sec:anatomy}): squared increments are logarithmically compressed before feature
normalization, and a set-conditioning block --- statistics pooled over the token set modulating each
feature channel, initialized to the identity --- adapts the normalization to each dataset.

\paragraph{Class rule.} Pair tokens are used exactly when the observed process is Markov; point tokens
otherwise. The rule was tested in both directions. A pair-token model transfers across Markov systems
(a model class trained on geometric Brownian motion re-trains cleanly on Ornstein--Uhlenbeck with
unchanged settings); on the Heston model, where the observed price is not Markov because volatility is
latent, pair and point models are statistically indistinguishable across all 25 evaluation cells, as the
factorization argument predicts --- the pair stencil confers an advantage only where the proposition
applies. The package selects the construction from a per-problem Markov flag.

\subsection{Learnable monotone reparametrization}
\label{sec:warp}

All tokenizations share one module: a monotone map applied to observed values and to time gaps,
\begin{equation}
  w(x) = \sum_{k} a_k \,\operatorname{sign}(x) \log\!\bigl(1 + |x| / s_k\bigr),
  \qquad a_k \ge 0,
  \label{eq:warp}
\end{equation}
with learnable weights $a_k$ over a geometric bank of scales $s_k$ (eight octaves by default). The mixture
interpolates continuously between the identity ($s_k$ large) and the logarithm ($s_k$ small), so the
network can learn the coordinate in which the process is well-conditioned --- for example the log-price
coordinate of geometric Brownian motion --- rather than having it prescribed.

The specific parametrization matters more than its expressive power, which is the reason we document it.
We first attempted a Yeo--Johnson layer \citep{yeo2000} with learnable $\lambda$: its parameter space
contains the logarithm exactly (at $\lambda = 0$), yet across repeated runs on GBM, gradient descent left
$\lambda$ near its initialization and the volatility bias persisted at $+0.48$ posterior standard
deviations --- the same failure as with no transform at all. The mixture \eqref{eq:warp}, whose components
are all active at initialization, reached the log coordinate reliably. Membership of the correct transform
in the hypothesis class is not sufficient; it must be reachable by gradient descent from the
initialization, and \eqref{eq:warp} is built so that it is.

\subsection{Observation times as attention phases}
\label{sec:trope}

Transformers encode order through positional embeddings; rotary embeddings \citep{su2024} encode the token
\emph{index} as a rotation of query--key pairs. For irregularly sampled data the index is not meaningful,
and using it is actively harmful in a way that is easy to miss. In an early configuration, training
subsampled long token sequences in place (leaving kept tokens at their original, spread-out indices) while
inference presented short, contiguously packed sequences. No individual component was incorrect, yet the
mismatch between the two positional distributions produced systematic biases of $1$--$3$ posterior
standard deviations at evaluation (Table~\ref{tab:designarms}). The failure is a hidden train/test
contract on token placement.

\amortix\ removes the contract by computing the attention rotation phases from \emph{physical time}: the
rotation angle of head-dimension $j$ for a token observed at time $t$ is $\omega_j t$ with a geometric
frequency bank $\omega_j$, so attention logits depend on time differences $t_i - t_j$ and not on storage
order. The encoder is then permutation-invariant by construction; we verify $10^{-8}$-level invariance
under random token permutations at float precision.

\subsection{Set size under normalized attention}
\label{sec:count}

Posterior width is governed by the number of observations ($\propto K^{-1/2}$ in regular models), but
softmax attention computes weighted \emph{means} over the set, and means are nearly invariant to $K$. The
deficit is measurable. We fit linear probes from the encoder's pooled representation to known sufficient
statistics of the GBM benchmark: every architectural variant recovers the posterior-mean statistics
($R^2 = 0.83$--$0.94$), but $R^2(\log K)$ separates the variants that produce correct widths from those
that do not --- $0.985$ for pair tokens, $0.957$ for index-based positions with contiguous packing,
$0.712$ for a continuous time-kernel attention bias, $0.498$ for pairwise edge-message attention --- and
the ranking of width errors follows the ranking of $R^2(\log K)$ exactly
(Table~\ref{tab:designarms}).

The repair requires no process knowledge, only making the set size available: either an explicit
$\log K$ input feature, or two learned register tokens \citep{darcet2024} appended to every set, which the
model uses to accumulate a count. On the hardest cell (dense designs, $K = 100$), the explicit feature
reduces the width ratio from $1.95$ to $1.49$ with bias unchanged at zero. The residual after the count
repair is a budget effect, not an architectural one (Sec.~\ref{sec:budget}). This analysis also explains an
otherwise puzzling observation: index-based positions with contiguous packing outperform metrically
correct continuous-time variants at dense designs, because with contiguous packing the maximal position
\emph{is} $K$ --- the ordinal embedding leaks the count.

\subsection{Velocity network}
\label{sec:vel}

The flow state $z_t$ is processed by self-attention over the $d$ parameter slots. The flow time $t$ and the
pooled data context enter every block through adaptive layer norms with zero-initialized modulation
(adaLN-Zero, \citealt{peebles2023}), so training starts from an identity-like map and departs only as
driven by data. Parameter-slot attention lets the model express posterior correlations (the ridge of the
Fisher--KPP posterior, the drift--volatility coupling of GBM) without a joint output head.

\section{Training procedures}
\label{sec:recipe}

\paragraph{Common settings.} Adam with cosine decay and linear warmup, base learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$,
batch 64, gradient clipping, EMA decay $0.999$. Each optimizer step draws fresh flow times $t$ (stratified)
and fresh base noise. Fixed-design models train on $n$ simulated datasets for a fixed number of epochs
(the gallery of Sec.~\ref{sec:gallery} uses $n = 40{,}000$ and 35 epochs).

\paragraph{Design amortization.} Training data are simulated once on a fine grid; observation designs are
then resampled \emph{from the stored fine trajectories at every optimizer step}. The procedure has three
components, each fixed by a controlled comparison on three systems (GBM, Ornstein--Uhlenbeck,
FitzHugh--Nagumo):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Fresh designs at every step.} Re-drawing each trajectory's observation subset at each step
  makes design memorization impossible and lets one simulation serve arbitrarily many designs. The
  alternative --- one fixed design per trajectory --- closes the dense-design error at long budgets but
  overfits the design set, producing overconfidence at sparse $K$ (width ratio $0.86$ against the exact
  reference).
  \item \emph{The design-size law.} $K$ is drawn $50\%$ log-uniform on $[K_{\min}, K_{\max}]$ and $50\%$
  uniform on the dense half of the range. Log-uniform sampling alone places $\approx 6\%$ of the mass at
  $K \ge 100$ and starves the dense regime, which appears as inflated widths precisely there; the mixture
  alone halves the dense-design excess (GBM $1.27 \to 1.14$, OU $1.34 \to 1.14$; the FitzHugh--Nagumo
  dense-$K$ SBC failure at $p < 10^{-3}$ disappears, $p = 0.54$).
  \item \emph{Budget scaling.} When higher accuracy is required, simulations and optimizer steps are scaled
  together at constant epochs; Sec.~\ref{sec:budget} shows the residuals then follow power laws in this
  single budget variable.
\end{enumerate}
With this procedure, the GBM posterior width matches the exact per-design posterior to $5$--$7\%$
uniformly over $K = 5, 9, 20, 100$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:widthk}).

\section{Evaluation methodology}
\label{sec:protocol}

Two instruments with different roles are used throughout, plus a diagnostic probe.

\subsection{Simulation-based calibration as a screen}

SBC \citep{talts2018} tests a necessary condition: for $m^\ast \sim p(m)$ and $\D \sim p(\D \mid m^\ast)$,
the rank of each component of $m^\ast$ among posterior samples must be uniform. We run SBC per parameter
(uniformity test on 500 simulated datasets with 200 posterior draws each unless stated; $p > 0.05$ passes)
and report coverage of central $50\%$ and $90\%$ intervals.

The power of the test at this size is limited, and because our conclusions depend on knowing that limit,
we measured it: on cells where an exact reference is available, SBC at $300$--$500$ datasets fails to
reject posterior width ratios up to $\approx 1.3$, and its per-cell $p$-values are unstable between
retrainings when residual biases are in the range $0.1$--$0.2$ posterior standard deviations. SBC also
cannot penalize a posterior that ignores the data and returns the prior; the prior-mean and ridge controls
of Sec.~\ref{sec:baselines-tiers} exist for that reason. SBC is therefore used as a cheap screen, never as
a verdict.

\subsection{Reference posteriors as the record}
\label{sec:refs}

Wherever a tractable likelihood exists, we compare distributions on the same observed points using two
statistics per parameter: the signed error of the posterior mean in units of the reference posterior
standard deviation (\emph{bias}) and the ratio of standard deviations (\emph{width}; $1$ is exact, $> 1$
conservative, $< 1$ overconfident). References are of three kinds (full details in
Appendix~\ref{app:refs}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Closed form.} The linear-Gaussian benchmark (exact Gaussian posterior) and geometric Brownian
  motion, whose log-returns give a conjugate normal--inverse-$\chi^2$ posterior importance-corrected to the
  box prior.
  \item \emph{Exact-likelihood MCMC.} An adaptive random-walk Metropolis sampler
  \citep[after][]{haario2001}: proposal scale adapted to a $0.25$ acceptance target during burn-in,
  proposal shape replaced by the empirical covariance Cholesky factor rescaled to $2.38/\sqrt{d}$,
  adaptation frozen before the retained phase, out-of-box proposals rejected (which samples the
  box-truncated target exactly). Likelihoods are exact per-gap transition densities: Gaussian
  Euler--Maruyama chain transitions for OU (matching the simulator that generated the data, plus the
  stationary density of the informative initial state), a Poisson-mixture transition density for the
  Merton jump-diffusion, log-normal residuals around the Bateman curve for pharmacokinetics, and a
  Gaussian observation model around the deterministic PDE solve for Fisher--KPP --- in the last case one
  likelihood evaluation is one PDE solve.
  \item \emph{Chain validation.} A reference is used only after validation. Every MCMC reference in this
  report is run at least twice from well-separated starting points, and the between-chain discrepancy of
  posterior means (in posterior-sd units) is reported with the result it certifies. Where a closed form
  and a chain both exist, the chains reproduce it to a worst mean discrepancy of $0.099$~sd and width
  ratios $0.91$--$1.03$ over 24 comparisons.
\end{itemize}
Twice during development the screen and the record contradicted each other; both times the contradiction
exposed an implementation error (a padding mask leaking into set-level statistics; the positional contract
of Sec.~\ref{sec:trope}). We consider the redundancy a feature of the methodology rather than an
inefficiency.

\subsection{Encoder probing}
\label{sec:probing}

To separate encoder failures from flow failures, we fit ridge regressions from the pooled encoder output
to known sufficient statistics of the problem. High probe $R^2$ with poor posterior accuracy localizes the
defect downstream of extraction. In the Merton analysis of Sec.~\ref{sec:anatomy}, the failing
configuration extracted robust volatility statistics \emph{better} than the healthy one
($R^2 = 0.967$ on truncated realized variance), redirecting the repair from the encoder's capacity to the
stability of its input representation.

\paragraph{Controls and classical baselines.}
\label{sec:baselines-tiers}
Every result is read against: (i) a prior-mean predictor (exactly $25\%$ mean absolute error under a

\subsection{Residuals scale as power laws in the training budget}
\label{sec:budget}

Two residual defects remained anywhere in the suite after the architectural work: inflated width on the
hardest Merton cell, and along-ridge bias on Fisher--KPP. Both were driven down by scaling simulations and
optimizer steps together at fixed model size (Fig.~\ref{fig:budget}). The Merton width excess follows
$\propto 1/\text{budget}$ ($0.246 \to 0.120 \to 0.059$ at $\times 1, \times 3, \times 6$); the KPP ridge
bias follows $\approx \text{budget}^{-1/2}$ ($+0.41 \to +0.23 \to +0.14$~sd along the ridge, with the
sharply identified front-speed component decaying in parallel). Two negative controls exclude the obvious
alternatives: doubling network width and depth at fixed budget changes the Merton cell by less than
$0.02$ in width ratio ($1.103$ vs $1.120$ at $\times 3$), and refining the sampling ODE solver from 20
midpoint steps to 60 RK4 steps changes nothing measurable. The weakly identified posterior direction
carries the weakest regression signal in \eqref{eq:cfm} and converges last; we find no evidence of a
floor within the measured range.

\subsection{Comparison with existing implementations}
\label{sec:headtohead}

\paragraph{Protocol.} We compare against the directly comparable amortized estimators available in
maintained packages: NPE with its default masked autoregressive flow \citep{papamakarios2017} and FMPE
\citep{wildberger2023} from \texttt{sbi}~0.27 \citep{boelts2024}; the coupling-flow and flow-matching
networks of BayesFlow~2.0 \citep{radev2020}; and, because it is the closest architecture to ours in the
literature, a port of the Simformer \citep{gloeckler2024} built from its authors' minimal example
(transformer--diffusion over the joint, random condition masks, their architecture and SDE settings, full
$75{,}000$-step training). All arms receive the same simulation budget ($20{,}000$ draws from the same
prior and simulator), run with their package defaults and their own stopping rules, and are scored on the
same test datasets against the same reference posteriors, using the bias and width statistics of
Sec.~\ref{sec:refs}. Because these estimators consume fixed-length vectors, the comparison uses fixed
observation designs. Three systems are used, chosen to separate the possible explanations of any
performance gap: a multiplicative process where input conditioning is hostile (GBM, exact conjugate
reference), an additive process where it is comparatively benign (OU, exact-likelihood MCMC reference,
two validated chains per dataset), and a PDE where the reference itself is expensive (Fisher--KPP, one
PDE solve per likelihood evaluation). Where informative, external arms are additionally given
hand-derived inputs --- log-prices, or per-gap log-returns, the exact coordinates of the GBM likelihood
factorization --- to separate the effect of the input representation from the generative engine.

\paragraph{Multiplicative process (GBM).} Table~\ref{tab:gbmcomp} and Figs.~\ref{fig:bars},
\ref{fig:posteriors} give the results; timing columns are wall-clock on one laptop CPU (Apple M-series;
Simformer rows on an H200 GPU, so their timings are not comparable with the rest while their accuracy
rows are). Three regularities are visible. First, with raw prices as input, every external configuration
inflates the volatility posterior --- widths $1.85$ (BayesFlow coupling) to $3.16$ (Simformer port) ---
irrespective of the generative engine; \texttt{sbi} itself emits a warning about precision loss during
z-scoring on this data, which is a fair description of the mechanism (Sec.~\ref{sec:anatomy}). Second,
supplying hand-derived coordinates does not produce convergent behavior: with identical log-return inputs,
NPE remains $44\%$ over-dispersed, FMPE is nearly exact ($1.09$), both BayesFlow arms become overconfident
(widths $0.42$--$0.85$), and the Simformer port acquires a $-0.48$~sd bias. Each of these defects is
invisible without an exact reference, and each lies within or near the blind region of SBC at practical
sizes (Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol}). Third, at fixed input coordinates the flow-matching engine outperforms
the autoregressive flow ($1.09$ vs $1.44$), consistent with the engine choice of Sec.~\ref{sec:method},
but no external arm matches the learnable representation operating on raw data ($1.05$). The verdicts are
stable across devices and seeds: re-running the sbi and BayesFlow arms on an H200 GPU with fresh seeds

\paragraph{Scope of the comparison.} Every external arm ran at its package defaults under our simulation

The comparison is designed to measure exactly that dependence: what accuracy is delivered when the input

Sec.~\ref{sec:design-amortization} is not exercised here at all, since the external estimators consume
fixed-length inputs by default.

\subsection{Computational cost}
\label{sec:cost}

The default model trains in minutes to tens of minutes per system: on one H200 GPU, a variable-design zoo
system at base budget ($20{,}000$ simulations, $12{,}000$ steps) takes 8--12 minutes, and the $\times 6$
budget behind the sharpest numbers of Fig.~\ref{fig:budget} takes about 50 minutes; on a laptop CPU the
fixed-design gallery systems take 8--45 minutes. Inference draws $2{,}000$ posterior samples for a
dataset in $426$\,ms on the laptop CPU and $72$\,ms (batched; $135$\,ms single) on the GPU. GPU and CPU
runs produce bit-identical posteriors because all stochastic generation is kept on a CPU random number
generator by design, so a device change cannot silently alter results. For orientation against
non-amortized inference: on GBM, whose likelihood is a three-line formula, adaptive Metropolis costs
$42$\,ms per dataset and amortization buys nothing for a single dataset; on Fisher--KPP, where one
likelihood evaluation is one PDE solve ($16$\,ms), the same reference sampler costs minutes per dataset
(measured in Table~\ref{tab:kppcomp}); and for Heston or Hodgkin--Huxley no likelihood formula is
available at any price. The regime where amortization pays is determined by the likelihood cost, the
number of datasets, and --- as Table~\ref{tab:gbmcomp} indicates --- by whether a trustworthy input
representation for a conditional density estimator is available without one.

\section{Failure analysis: conditioning of the encoder input}
\label{sec:anatomy}

Every accuracy failure encountered during the development of this package --- including, we argue, the
external failures of Table~\ref{tab:gbmcomp} --- reduces to a single cause appearing in three forms: the
conditioning of the numbers presented to the set encoder. We document the three forms with their
measurements and repairs; all three repairs are learnable modules rather than problem-specific
transformations.

\paragraph{Form 1: floating multiplicative scale.} On GBM, raw price tokens force the network to divide
squared increments by squared levels internally; with global standardization this manifests as a
volatility bias of $+0.48$ posterior sd (confirmed against the closed-form posterior) that no amount of
training removes. The repair is the monotone mixture reparametrization of Sec.~\ref{sec:warp}, which
learns the log coordinate; the Yeo--Johnson ablation there shows why the parametrization, not the
hypothesis class, is the operative choice. External estimators exhibit the same form on the same data
(Table~\ref{tab:gbmcomp}, raw-price rows).

\paragraph{Form 2: cardinality blindness.} Normalized attention erases set size, which controls posterior
width under design amortization; the probe measurements and the $\log K$/register-token repairs are given
in Sec.~\ref{sec:count}, with the arm-by-arm consequences in Table~\ref{tab:designarms}.

\paragraph{Form 3: heavy tails.} On the Merton jump-diffusion, squared increments have sample standard
deviation $3.1 \times 10^4$ against a diffusion signal of order $10^{-2}$--$1$; running feature
normalization never converges, and the flow trains against a drifting representation, producing a
$+1.12$~sd volatility bias. Encoder probing (Sec.~\ref{sec:probing}) shows the information is extracted
--- the failing configuration encodes robust volatility statistics better than the healthy one --- so the
repair targets representation stability: logarithmic compression of increment features (near-identity on
clean diffusions since $\log(1+x) \approx x$ for small $x$) restores all 25 evaluation cells. A precision
follow-up against the Poisson-mixture reference then isolated a smaller ($+0.27$~sd) residual whose
mechanism is a jump-classification threshold that floats with the unknown volatility; a per-dataset
set-conditioning module (pooled statistics modulating feature channels, initialized to the identity)
removes it as effectively as a hand-crafted per-dataset rescaling ($+0.127 \pm 0.055$ vs
$+0.094 \pm 0.054$~sd, statistically indistinguishable). This was one of three such paired comparisons in
the project (log coordinate, set size, robust scaling); in all three the learnable module matched the
hand-crafted repair once initialized at the working configuration. We summarize the pattern as a design
principle: the hand-crafted fix identifies the mechanism and the initialization; the learnable module
placed there is what ships.

Three implementation conditions proved necessary for the set-conditioning module and are recorded here
because each was found through a failure: the modulation must be zero-initialized (training must start
from the working baseline); it must act after feature normalization, not before (the reverse ordering
re-creates the representation drift it is meant to cure); and pooled set statistics must exclude padding
positions (a masking error here made padded and packed representations of the same dataset inequivalent,
detected only because SBC and the exact-reference probe disagreed).

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

The evidence assembled here supports a specific claim: for dynamical-systems inverse problems, the input
representation of an amortized posterior estimator is not a preprocessing detail but the dominant
accuracy factor, and it can be made part of the model rather than part of the problem specification. The
comparison of Sec.~\ref{sec:headtohead} shows the cost of the alternative --- fixed representations with
global standardization --- across four generative engines, on a problem where the correct answer is known
in closed form. The OU control separates the mechanisms: on additive data the bias of the external
estimators disappears and their width inflation drops by roughly a factor of two but does not vanish, so
the conditioning pathology accounts for the dominant part of the failure and a smaller
representation-learning gap accounts for the rest.

Limitations. (i) The remaining inaccuracies of the suite decay as measured power laws in training compute
(Fig.~\ref{fig:budget}); we have not searched for methods that improve the exponents. (ii) The power
limits of SBC imply that small residuals can only be certified where an exact or validated reference
exists; systems without tractable likelihoods inherit the weaker screen, and carrying reference-equipped
systems alongside them in one suite is our current mitigation. (iii) The comparison of
Sec.~\ref{sec:headtohead} uses package defaults; it measures the out-of-the-box regime, not the ceiling
of any method. (iv) Bayesian experimental design --- the intended consumer of design amortization --- is
not yet demonstrated; the training procedure of Sec.~\ref{sec:recipe} was built so that the required
object $p(m \mid S)$ is already coherent across subsets $S$. (v) No real-data study with posterior
predictive checks is included yet. (vi) All priors are uniform on boxes; the probit normalization
\eqref{eq:probit} extends to any factorized prior with tractable CDFs, but correlated priors would
require a different normalization.

All SDEs are integrated by Euler--Maruyama on the stated fine grid; ODEs by RK4 (Hodgkin--Huxley by a
semi-implicit scheme for the gating variables); the PDE by finite differences. Fixed-design observations
are token sets over the union of the stated channels; ``fast/slow'' channels observe every step over a
short window and every $j$-th step over the horizon respectively. Priors are uniform on the stated boxes.
Classical estimators (\texttt{sota()}) are listed per system.

\paragraph{Linear-Gaussian.} $y = Am + \varepsilon$, $\varepsilon \sim \N(0, 0.5^2 I_6)$, with a fixed
correlated design matrix $A \in \R^{6 \times 4}$; $m \in [-3, 3]^4$. Exact Gaussian posterior; the
classical estimator is the posterior mean itself. This system checks the flow engine in isolation from
the encoder.

\paragraph{Ornstein--Uhlenbeck.} $dX = -\theta X\,dt + \sigma\,dW$, stationary start
$X_0 \sim \N(0, \sigma^2/2\theta)$; $dt = 0.02$, $500$ steps; channels: fast $(1 \times 50)$, slow
$(20 \times 24)$. Prior $\theta \in [0.3, 3]$, $\sigma \in [0.2, 1.5]$. Classical: exact MLE from
per-gap Gaussian transitions.

\paragraph{Geometric Brownian motion.} $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW$, $S_0 = 1$; $dt = 0.01$, $500$
steps (five ``years''); channels: fast $(1 \times 60)$, slow $(10 \times 40)$. Prior
$\mu \in [-0.2, 0.4]$, $\sigma \in [0.1, 0.6]$. Classical: exact MLE on log-returns. Reference: conjugate
posterior (Appendix~\ref{app:refs}).

\paragraph{Cox--Ingersoll--Ross.} $dX = a(b - X)\,dt + \sigma\sqrt{X}\,dW$; $dt = 0.02$, $500$ steps;
channels as OU. Prior $a \in [0.3, 3]$, $b \in [0.3, 1.5]$, $\sigma \in [0.1, 0.5]$. Classical: Euler
pseudo-likelihood MLE \citep{cox1985}.

\paragraph{Double well.} $dX = (\theta_1 X - \theta_2 X^3)\,dt + \sigma\,dW$; $dt = 0.01$, $1000$ steps,
noise strong enough for barrier crossings; channels: fast $(1 \times 80)$, slow $(25 \times 36)$. Prior
$\theta_{1,2} \in [0.5, 3]$, $\sigma \in [0.4, 1.2]$. Classical: Kramers--Moyal regression.

\paragraph{Stochastic Lotka--Volterra.} Prey/predator pair with multiplicative noise,
$dx_1 = (\alpha x_1 - \beta x_1 x_2)\,dt + 0.1 x_1\,dW_1$,
$dx_2 = (\delta x_1 x_2 - \gamma x_2)\,dt + 0.1 x_2\,dW_2$; both components observed; $dt = 0.01$, $600$
steps; channels: fast $(1 \times 50)$, slow $(15 \times 40)$. Prior $\alpha, \gamma \in [0.8, 1.5]$,
$\beta, \delta \in [0.4, 1.2]$. Classical: multi-start nonlinear least squares.

\paragraph{FitzHugh--Nagumo.} $\dot v = v - v^3/3 - w + I + \text{noise}$,
$\dot w = \epsilon(v + a - b w)$; only $v$ observed at 25 evenly spaced times over horizon 40
($dt = 0.05$), observation noise $0.05$. Prior $a, b \in [0.5, 0.9]$, $\epsilon \in [0.05, 0.3]$,
$I \in [0, 0.5]$. Classical: multi-start nonlinear least squares on the deterministic solve.

\paragraph{SEIR with mortality.} Five-compartment stochastic epidemic (two-phase contact rate $\beta_1
\to \beta_2$, incubation $\alpha$, recovery $\gamma_r$, mortality $\gamma_d$); infected and deceased
compartments observed at 20 days over a 60-day horizon ($dt = 0.1$), observation noise $0.004$. Prior in
Table~\ref{tab:pvals}. Classical: nonlinear least squares on $(I, D)$.

\paragraph{SINDy-SDE.} $dX = (c_0 + c_1 X + c_2 X^2 + c_3 X^3)\,dt + \sigma\,dW$ \citep[in the spirit
of][]{brunton2016}; $dt = 0.01$, $1000$ steps; channels: fast $(1 \times 80)$, slow $(20 \times 45)$.
Prior $c_0, c_2 \in [-0.5, 0.5]$, $c_1 \in [-2, 0]$, $c_3 \in [-1, -0.1]$, $\sigma \in [0.3, 0.8]$.
Classical: SINDy-style least squares on Kramers--Moyal estimates.

\paragraph{Heston (variable designs).} $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sqrt{v}\,S\,dW_1$,
$dv = \kappa(\bar v - v)\,dt + \xi \sqrt{v}\,dW_2$, $\operatorname{corr}(dW_1, dW_2) = \rho$
\citep{heston1993}; price observed, volatility latent; $dt = 0.01$, $500$ steps, designs of
$K \in [2, 128]$ random times. Prior $\mu \in [-0.1, 0.3]$, $\kappa \in [0.5, 5]$,
$\bar v \in [0.02, 0.3]$, $\xi \in [0.1, 1]$, $\rho \in [-0.9, 0]$.

\paragraph{Merton jump-diffusion (variable designs).} $dS/S = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW + d\bigl(\sum_i (e^{J_i}
- 1)\bigr)$ with Poisson jump rate $\lambda$ and $J_i \sim \N(\mu_J, s_J^2)$ \citep{merton1976};
$dt = 0.01$, $500$ steps, $K \in [2, 128]$. Prior $\mu \in [-0.1, 0.3]$, $\sigma \in [0.1, 0.5]$,
$\lambda \in [0.2, 3]$, $\mu_J \in [-0.3, 0.3]$, $s_J \in [0.05, 0.4]$. Reference: Poisson-mixture
transition density (Appendix~\ref{app:refs}).

\paragraph{H\'enon--Heiles (variable designs).} Two-dimensional Hamiltonian oscillator with the coupling
potential used in \citet{lubich2015}, frequencies $(\omega_1, \omega_2)$ and coupling $\lambda$; $q_1$
observed at random times with noise; $dt = 0.05$, $800$ steps, $K \in [4, 64]$. Prior
$\omega_{1,2} \in [0.7, 1.3]$, $\lambda \in [0.02, 0.22]$.

\paragraph{Hodgkin--Huxley (variable designs).} Standard four-dimensional membrane model
\citep{hodgkin1952}; voltage observed at random times; parameters $(g_{\mathrm{Na}}, g_K, g_L, I)$ with
prior $[60, 180] \times [10, 50] \times [0.05, 0.5] \times [4, 12]$; $3000$ integration steps,
$K \in [4, 96]$.

\paragraph{Pharmacokinetics (variable designs).} One-compartment oral dosing (Bateman function),
concentration $c(t) = \frac{D_0 k_a}{V(k_a - k_e)}\bigl(e^{-k_e t} - e^{-k_a t}\bigr)$ with dose
$D_0 = 500$; log-normal assay noise ($10\%$); horizon 24 h, $K \in [3, 64]$ irregular draws. Prior
$k_a \in [0.5, 4]$, $k_e \in [0.05, 0.4]$, $V \in [20, 100]$. Reference: log-normal likelihood MCMC.

\paragraph{Fisher--KPP (variable designs).} $\partial_t \theta = D\,\partial_{xx} \theta + r\,\theta(1 -
\theta)$ on $[0, 1]$ with reflecting boundaries, 64-point finite-difference grid, Gaussian initial bump;
three fixed sensors at $x = 16/63, 32/63, 48/63$ observed at random (time, sensor) pairs with noise
$0.02$; $dt = 0.01$, $200$ steps, $K \in [4, 96]$. Prior $D \in [0.001, 0.01]$, $r \in [2, 10]$.
Reference: Gaussian observation model around the deterministic solve; one likelihood evaluation is one
PDE solve.

\section{Reference posteriors}
\label{app:refs}

\paragraph{GBM conjugate posterior.} With observations at indices $i_0 = 0 < i_1 < \dots$ of the fine
grid, log-returns $r_k = \log(S_{i_{k+1}}/S_{i_k})$ over gaps $\tau_k$ are independent
$\N\bigl((\mu - \sigma^2/2)\tau_k, \sigma^2 \tau_k\bigr)$. In the coordinates
$(b, \sigma^2) = (\mu - \sigma^2/2, \sigma^2)$ the model is a Gaussian location--scale family with
sufficient statistics $T = \sum_k \tau_k$, $R_1 = \sum_k r_k$, and
$SS_w = \sum_k r_k^2 / \tau_k - R_1^2 / T$; draws from the standard conjugate posterior
($\sigma^2 = SS_w / \chi^2_{n-1}$, $b \mid \sigma^2 \sim \N(R_1/T, \sigma^2/T)$) are transformed to
$(\mu, \sigma)$ and importance-resampled to the uniform box prior with the appropriate Jacobian weight.
The sampler was validated against exact-likelihood MCMC on the same designs: over 24 comparisons, worst
posterior-mean discrepancy $0.099$~sd, width ratios $0.91$--$1.03$.

\paragraph{Adaptive Metropolis.} Random-walk Metropolis on the box, proposal
$x' = x + s\,C\,\xi$, $\xi \sim \N(0, I)$. During burn-in (default $\max(500, n/2)$ iterations) the scalar
$s$ is adapted toward a $0.25$ acceptance rate in blocks of 50 with a decaying Robbins--Monro gain, and
once sufficient burn-in states are collected the shape $C$ is replaced by the Cholesky factor of the
empirical covariance rescaled by $2.38/\sqrt{d}$ \citep{haario2001}; adaptation is frozen for the retained
phase. Out-of-box proposals are rejected, which samples the box-truncated target exactly since the
proposal is symmetric. Likelihoods: per-gap Euler--Maruyama Gaussian transitions for OU (matching the
generative process, plus the stationary density of $X_0$); the exact GBM log-return density; the
Poisson-mixture transition density for Merton (truncated at the numerically negligible jump count);
log-normal residuals around the Bateman curve; a Gaussian observation model around the deterministic PDE
solve for Fisher--KPP. Every chain used as a reference is paired with a second chain from a
well-separated start; between-chain posterior-mean discrepancies are reported alongside the results they
certify (Sec.~\ref{sec:headtohead}).
